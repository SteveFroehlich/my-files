
If I were ranking by expected impact on a typical senior knowledge worker’s real-world performance (rather than strict evidentiary strength), I’d probably still put:

1. Sleep
2. Chronic stress / uncertainty
3. Too many simultaneous commitments
4. Context switching
5. High-cost open loops

#### deeper dive 

If we rank by strength of clinical/scientific evidence linking the factor to cognitive performance, I’d reorder and relabel it like this:

1. Sleep deficits

Evidence strength: Very high

- One of the most replicated findings in cognitive science.
- Strong causal evidence from randomized and sleep-deprivation studies.
- Impacts attention, working memory, executive function, learning, reaction time, and decision-making.

2. Chronic stress / perceived stress

Evidence strength: High

- Strong evidence from laboratory stress studies and longitudinal research.
- Associated with impaired working memory, executive function, cognitive flexibility, and attention.

3. Persistent uncertainty around important outcomes

Evidence strength: Moderate to high

- Often studied under stress, lack of control, anticipatory anxiety, and uncertainty rather than as a standalone construct.
- The evidence that uncertainty is cognitively costly is substantial, though the literature is more fragmented.

4. Context switching / interruptions

Evidence strength: Moderate

- Strong experimental evidence that interruptions and task switching reduce performance and increase error rates.
- Less commonly studied in clinical settings, more often in human factors, organizational psychology, and cognitive psychology.

5. Too many simultaneous commitments

Evidence strength: Moderate to low

- Related to cognitive load theory and workload research.
- Well supported conceptually, but harder to isolate and measure as a single causal factor.

6. High-cost open loops

Evidence strength: Low to moderate

- Supported indirectly through research on unfinished goals, prospective memory, rumination, and the Zeigarnik effect.
- Much weaker evidence than sleep or stress

## Mediation 

Consistency appears to matter more than session length. A short practice performed most days is generally associated with better outcomes than occasional long sessions.  

As for clinically established thresholds, the evidence is surprisingly messy.

What has reasonably strong support

- ~10 minutes/day is enough to produce measurable benefits in many studies, including reductions in anxiety, stress, and improvements in mindfulness. Going from 10 to 20 minutes often produces only small additional gains.  
- Benefits are commonly observed after 2–8 weeks of regular practice. Many of the classic mindfulness programs run for 8 weeks, while some studies observe effects after just 2 weeks.  
- Brief interventions can show measurable effects even after a few sessions or a single session, though those effects are typically smaller and less durable.  

What is not established

There is no universally accepted minimum effective dose such as:

- “12 minutes/day for 30 consecutive days”
- “20 minutes/day for 8 weeks”

The literature has not converged on a single threshold.  

If I were forced to give an evidence-based target

For a busy knowledge worker:

- Minimum viable: 10 min/day
- Duration: 6–8 weeks before judging effectiveness
- Primary goal: consistency rather than increasing duration

That recommendation is supported more strongly than jumping immediately to 30–60 minute sessions.  

One interesting recent finding is that in real-world meditation data, practice frequency was a stronger predictor of improvement than session duration, which is about as direct a validation of the “consistency matters more than length” idea as I’ve seen.

## Reduce open loops
Less clinical evidence for this

High-cost open loops

Unresolved + ambiguous.

- Should we change strategy?
- Is this partnership working?
- Am I making the right decision?
- How do we solve this problem?

Low-cost open loops

Unresolved + clearly defined.

- Email Bob.
- Buy snacks.
- Schedule meeting.
- Review document Friday.

Tools
- Task manager (Todoist, Obsidian tasks, etc.)
- Calendar
- Notes/knowledge system (Obsidian)
- Project plans
- Checklists
- Decision logs

  

Processes

1. Capture everything quickly.
2. Clarify the next action.
3. Make decisions explicitly.
4. Break large questions into smaller decisions.
5. Schedule or delegate immediately.
6. Maintain a trusted single source of truth.
7. Review regularly and close stale loops.
8. Limit active projects/WIP.

A useful heuristic:

An open loop should become either:

- a decision,
- a next action,
- a calendar event,
- a delegated task,
- or be dropped.
